What I Wish I Knew at 22

What I’d tell myself at 22 about money

Money is not mainly about how much you can make. It is about not getting surprised, and about the gap between what comes in and what goes out.

By Hemang Champaneria·November 2026·4 min read

If I could sit down with myself at 22, I would not start with a lecture about ambition. I was not short on ambition. I would start with a quieter fact: money is not mainly about how much you can make. It is about not getting surprised, and about the gap between what comes in and what goes out.

At 22, I treated a good week as proof that things were fine. A busy stretch can still leave you short if the money leaves faster than it arrives. Sales are not profit. A full calendar is not a reserve. The number that matters is what remains after you have paid the people you owe, including yourself.

I would tell myself to learn the numbers early, and to look at them when nothing is wrong, not only when something feels tight. You do not need to become an accountant. You do need to know, without asking someone else, what came in last month, what went out, what you still owe, and how long you could last if income slowed. If you cannot answer those four questions, you are running on hope. Hope is not a plan.

Pay yourself a modest, regular amount, the way you would pay any other bill. “Whatever is left” is, more often than not, nothing. If the work cannot support a small, steady payment to you, that is useful information. It is better to learn it while the amounts are still small.

Keep a reserve. A slow month will come. So will a repair, a late payment from someone else, or a week when you cannot be at work. This is not pessimism. It is how you stay in the game long enough for the good months to matter. Move a little money aside on a schedule, even when it feels too small to count. Small amounts, left alone, grow. That is not a slogan. It is arithmetic, and you can watch it happen.

I would tell myself to be careful with debt. Debt is a tool, not a personality, and not a measure of whether you are serious. Used with a clear purpose, and on terms you understand, it can help. Used to paper over a month that did not work, or to buy a life you have not yet earned, it becomes a second job you did not mean to take. If you cannot explain the terms in plain language, do not sign. If someone is in a hurry for your signature, slow down.

Keep business money and personal money apart from the first day, even if the business is only you. When the accounts are mixed, you cannot tell how you are doing, and a messy year becomes a much harder one. Open the separate account. Pay yourself from it. Keep the receipts in one place. It is unglamorous, and it will save you.

Lifestyle will try to rise with every good season. Let some of the good season go by without changing your life. The point of money, if it has a point beyond meeting your obligations, is options — the ability to say no, to wait, to fix something properly, to help someone without resentment. Display is expensive, and it is usually rented. Options last.

If you start to put money away for later, start simply. You do not need a clever strategy. You need time, patience, and a basic understanding of how compounding works. Avoid tips, hot names, and anyone who promises an easy return. There is a reason official, plain-language guides exist on this. Use them. The most expensive investing mistakes I have watched people make were not failures of intelligence. They were failures of patience, or of trust placed in the wrong voice.

Write down your recurring costs a few times a year. A few small subscriptions, a fee you stopped noticing, a habit that grew by degrees — none look like a problem on their own. Together they can erase the reserve you are trying to build.

Ask for help before the books are a mess, not after. A bookkeeper, a tax professional, or a mentor who has already made the ordinary mistakes will cost you less than a year of guessing. Being careful with money is not the same as being small-hearted. You can be generous and still know your numbers. In fact, you will be a more reliable kind of generous, because your promises will clear.

One last thing I would tell myself, because it took me too long to treat it as a money lesson: pay other people on time. Vendors, staff, the person who did a small job for you. Late payment is not a rounding error. It is a message about whose time you think matters. It is also a reputation, and reputation is a financial asset, whether or not it appears on a statement.

One thing to try this week

Write down three numbers, honestly: what came in last month, what went out, and how many weeks you could keep going if income stopped tomorrow. Then make one small fix — a transfer into savings, a bill set to pay itself on time, or a subscription you do not use. One set of numbers, one fix, this week.

Useful reading

  • Introduction to Investing. Investor.gov, from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. A plain-language starting point for saving and investing, without sales pitches.
  • Compound Interest Calculator. Investor.gov. A simple way to see how small amounts, left alone, can grow.
  • Consumer Tools. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Clear guides to bank accounts, credit reports, and other everyday money questions.
  • Manage your finances. U.S. Small Business Administration. Practical guidance for tracking and understanding the money in a small operation.
  • The Psychology of Money, by Morgan Housel. The publisher’s official book page. Short essays on how behavior, not just arithmetic, shapes a financial life.
