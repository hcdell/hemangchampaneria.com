Kindness is a business skill

Hemang Champaneria

I used to think of kindness as a personal quality, something you either had or you did not, and something that lived outside the work. I do not think that anymore. Kindness is a skill. You can practice it. And in a small operation, it is one of the most practical skills you can have.

Kindness is not softness, and it is not the same as being liked. You can be kind and still say no. You can be kind and still hold a high standard. You can be kind and still tell someone, clearly, that the work is not good enough yet. What you cannot do is use pressure as an excuse to make a person feel small. The work still has to get done. The person in front of you still has to be able to do it tomorrow.

In a small operation, there is nowhere to hide a sharp moment. There is no large office to smooth it over, and no real distance between the person who snapped and the person who heard it. A short, hard word in a rushed hour travels. People repeat it to themselves on the way home. They decide, quietly, how much of the truth they will bring you next time.

I have learned to watch for that. When people stop bringing you the small problems, it is rarely because the small problems have gone away. It is because they have decided it is safer to keep them. Kindness, practiced steadily, does the opposite. It makes it cheaper to tell the truth early. A missed order, a short count, a vendor who is slipping, a customer who left unhappy — those things are inexpensive when they are one day old and expensive when they are a month old. Fear hides them. Respect surfaces them.

Kindness shows up in ordinary work, which is the only place it really counts. You greet people. You explain a mistake without humiliation. You pay on time. You return a call when you said you would. You give a clear answer instead of leaving someone waiting, hoping the question will disappear. You thank a person for a specific thing they did, not for “all their hard work” in a sentence that could have been sent to anyone.

With customers, the way you handle a complaint often matters more than the mistake that caused it. Most people can accept that something went wrong. What they cannot accept is being treated as a nuisance for saying so. A calm apology, a straightforward fix, and a follow-up later — that is kindness, and it is also how you keep someone you would otherwise lose. The same is true with vendors, neighbors, and anyone who helps you do the work. People have long memories for who was decent when a delivery was late or a bill was confused.

With the people who work with you, a simple rule has served me well: give credit in public, and give correction in private. Do not use sarcasm as a way of managing anyone. If someone’s work suddenly slips, ask if they are all right before you decide they have gotten careless. You will be wrong sometimes, and the question still costs you nothing.

None of this requires a speech, a poster, or a statement of values. It requires consistency. Kindness that appears only when someone is watching is not kindness. It is a performance. People are very good at telling the difference. They watch how you treat the person with the least power in the room. They watch how you behave when you are tired. That is the version of you they will trust.

There is a version of “kindness” that is really avoidance: not having the hard conversation, letting poor work slide, saying yes because no would be uncomfortable. That helps no one. The person who is struggling does not get better. The rest of the team learns that standards are negotiable. The kind thing, in those moments, is to be clear and early, and to leave the other person with their dignity.

I think of kindness now the way I think of any other skill worth having. You practice it when it is easy so that you still have it when it is not. You get a little better by noticing the moments you regret, and doing them differently the next time. You will not be perfect. You do not have to be. You have to be steady enough that the people around you know what to expect.

One thing to try this week

Pick one hard conversation, or one tense moment you already know is coming. Before you speak, ask yourself: “How do I tell the truth here without making the other person smaller?” Then do that, once, on purpose. Afterward, notice what changed — in the other person, and in you.

Useful reading

  • Why Kindness Isn’t a Nice to Have. Harvard Business Review. A clear argument that kindness is not an extra, and that teams pay a real price when they treat it as one.
  • The Price of Incivility. Harvard Business Review. Research on how small acts of rudeness spread, and what they cost.
  • Kindness Makes You Happy, and Happiness Makes You Kind. Greater Good Science Center, UC Berkeley. A short look at the research on why ordinary kindness tends to reinforce itself.
  • Give and Take, by Adam Grant. The author’s official book page. A useful book on why people who help others carefully, not naively, tend to do well over time.
